# Defensiveness Playbook: Strategies by Level

This guide provides specific therapeutic strategies organized by the client's defensiveness level. Defensiveness is a natural response to perceived threat and is influenced by how the conversation is conducted. The therapist's approach should adapt fluidly based on the level of defensiveness observed.

---

## Defensiveness: None or Low

### Description

The client is open, willing to explore, and not perceiving threat. They may express curiosity about change, acknowledge problems without prompting, or actively engage in self-reflection. This is the ideal state for deepening motivation and strengthening change talk.

### Goals

- Strengthen and amplify emerging change talk
- Evoke deeper motivation and connection to values
- Build confidence that change is achievable
- Encourage commitment language (moving from "I might" to "I will")
- Explore the client's vision for their future

### Strategies

**1. Amplify Change Talk**
When the client says something that favors change, reflect it back with emphasis. Make their change talk louder and more visible to them.

**2. Evocative Questions**
Ask questions designed to pull out more change talk. Use importance, confidence, and readiness rulers. Explore values and vision.

**3. Looking Forward / Looking Back**
Help the client compare where they are now to where they want to be or where they've been. Both create motivation.

**4. Affirm Strengths and Resources**
Build self-efficacy by naming the capabilities and resources the client already has.

**5. Deepen the Conversation**
With low defensiveness, you can explore more vulnerable territory - relationships, identity, fears, hopes. Go where the client leads.

### Example Exchanges

**Exchange 1: Amplifying Change Talk**
Client: "I think I might be ready to try something different. The drinking isn't really working for me anymore."
Therapist: "Something has shifted for you. You're looking at drinking and seeing that it's not giving you what it used to. What's changed?"

**Exchange 2: Evoking Deeper Motivation**
Client: "I want to be healthier. My dad had liver problems and I don't want to end up like that."
Therapist: "Your dad's experience showed you something you don't want for yourself. When you imagine being healthier, what does that actually look like for you day to day?"

**Exchange 3: Looking Forward**
Client: "I've been thinking about what life would be like without the hangovers."
Therapist: "You've already started picturing it. Paint that picture for me. If the hangovers were gone, what does a typical Saturday morning look like?"

**Exchange 4: Affirming Strengths**
Client: "I managed to go the whole week without drinking. It wasn't easy but I did it."
Therapist: "A full week. You decided to do something hard, and you followed through. What got you through the tough moments?"

**Exchange 5: Deepening**
Client: "I want to be the kind of dad who's actually present. Not just there physically."
Therapist: "There's a difference between being in the room and being present, and you see that clearly. Tell me about a moment when you were truly present with your kids. What was that like?"

## Defensiveness: Moderate

### Description

The client shows signs of ambivalence and discomfort. They may minimize problems, rationalize drinking, compare themselves favorably to others, or give mixed signals ("I know it's bad, but..."). They're not hostile, but they're guarding against something. They may feel judged, pressured, or uncertain.

### Goals

- Reduce perceived threat while maintaining exploration
- Shift the balance from sustain talk toward change talk
- Use the client's ambivalence as a therapeutic tool
- Avoid pushing, which would increase defensiveness
- Create space for the client to argue for change themselves

### Strategies

**1. Double-Sided Reflections**
Capture both sides of the ambivalence. This shows the client you hear all of them, not just the part that supports change. Always put the change-talk side second (the side you want them to sit with).

**2. Explore Rather Than Challenge**
When the client minimizes or rationalizes, explore it with genuine curiosity rather than challenging it. Curiosity disarms; challenge provokes.

**3. Develop Discrepancy Gently**
Help the client notice gaps between their behavior and their values. Do this through questions, not statements. Never say "Don't you see the contradiction?" Instead, place the two things side by side and let the client make the connection.

**4. Emphasize Autonomy**
Moderate defensiveness often comes from feeling pressured. Regularly remind the client that the choice is theirs. This paradoxically increases openness.

**5. Use the Columbo Approach**
Appear curious and slightly confused rather than knowing and directive. "I'm a bit puzzled by something..." or "Help me understand..." This invites the client to explain, which often leads them to hear their own contradictions.

### Example Exchanges

**Exchange 1: Double-Sided Reflection**
Client: "I mean, yeah, I drink a lot. But it's not like I'm getting DUIs or losing my job."
Therapist: "Things haven't reached a crisis point, and at the same time, you just described your drinking as 'a lot.' Part of you knows it's more than you want it to be."

**Exchange 2: Exploring Minimization**
Client: "It's just beer. It's not like I'm doing shots of vodka at breakfast."
Therapist: "You see a clear difference between what you're doing and something more extreme. I'm curious - when you compare your drinking to what you think of as a 'real problem,' where do you draw the line?"

**Exchange 3: Gentle Discrepancy**
Client: "I'm a good father. I take care of my family. The drinking doesn't affect that."
Therapist: "Being a good dad is something you take seriously - I can hear that. You mentioned earlier that you missed your daughter's recital because you weren't feeling well that morning. How do those two things fit together for you?"

**Exchange 4: Autonomy Emphasis**
Client: "Everyone keeps telling me what to do. My wife, my doctor, now this."
Therapist: "You've had a lot of people weighing in, and it feels like your voice has gotten lost in all of it. At the end of the day, this is your life and your decision. What do YOU think about your drinking?"

**Exchange 5: Columbo Approach**
Client: "I only drink on weekends. It's totally under control."
Therapist: "Help me understand something. You said you only drink on weekends, and you also mentioned that the Tuesday night drinks with coworkers have become a regular thing. I might be mixing things up - can you walk me through what a typical week looks like?"

## Defensiveness: High

### Description

The client is actively resisting the conversation. They may be argumentative, hostile, dismissive, or shut down. They perceive significant threat - to their autonomy, identity, or self-image. They may feel forced to be there, judged, or labeled. They may use denial, blame, anger, or withdrawal as protective mechanisms.

### Goals

- De-escalate immediately - safety and trust come first
- Reduce perceived threat to near zero
- Avoid anything that could be interpreted as confrontation
- Preserve the relationship above all else
- Create the conditions for the client to return to openness on their own timeline

### Strategies

**1. Amplified Reflection**
Slightly overstate the client's position (without sarcasm). This technique works because when the client hears their position stated in a more extreme form, they often soften it themselves. Use carefully - overdo it and it becomes mocking.

**2. Shifting Focus**
Move the conversation away from the point of resistance. If alcohol is the battleground, stop fighting on that ground. Talk about something else - their life, their goals, what's going on at work - and let the topic of drinking re-emerge naturally.

**3. Reframing**
Offer an alternative interpretation of something the client has said. Not to argue, but to open a different way of seeing it. The client can take it or leave it.

**4. Coming Alongside**
Agree with the client. Take their side. This is counterintuitive but powerful. When the therapist stops being the opponent, the client no longer needs to fight.

**5. Emphasize Personal Choice and Control**
The most powerful de-escalation tool. When defensiveness is high, the client feels cornered. Explicitly giving them back their power reduces the need to fight for it.

### Example Exchanges

**Exchange 1: Amplified Reflection**
Client: "I don't have any kind of problem. Zero. I'm totally fine."
Therapist: "There's absolutely nothing about your drinking that concerns you at all. Everything is exactly the way you want it."
Client: "Well... I mean, it's not perfect. But it's not a problem."
Therapist: "It's not perfect. Tell me about the 'not perfect' part."

**Exchange 2: Shifting Focus**
Client: "I'm not going to sit here and talk about my drinking. You people are all the same."
Therapist: "Fair enough. We don't have to talk about drinking at all. What IS going on in your life right now? What's on your mind today?"

**Exchange 3: Reframing**
Client: "My wife threatened to leave me over this. She's being completely unreasonable."
Therapist: "It sounds like she went to a pretty extreme place. People usually don't make threats like that unless they're scared of losing someone they love."

**Exchange 4: Coming Alongside**
Client: "Everyone needs to back off. It's my life, I'll do what I want."
Therapist: "You're right. It is your life, and nobody else gets to make these decisions for you. I'm not here to tell you what to do. I'm just here to listen, if you're up for it."

**Exchange 5: Emphasize Personal Choice**
Client: "You're going to tell me I need to stop drinking, right? That's what this is about?"
Therapist: "I'm not going to tell you to do anything. Whether you drink, how much you drink, whether you ever change anything - that's entirely up to you. I have no power over that, and I wouldn't want it. What I can do is have a conversation with you about whatever matters to you right now."

## Recognizing Defensiveness Shifts

### Signs Defensiveness Is Rising

- Shorter, more clipped responses
- Increased use of "but," "whatever," "I don't care"
- Crossing arms (in person), shifting topics, going quiet
- Blaming others, becoming argumentative
- Statements about wanting to leave or end the conversation
- Repeating "I'm fine" or "it's not a problem" with increasing intensity

### Signs Defensiveness Is Lowering

- Longer, more reflective responses
- Asking questions about change or options
- Softening language: "maybe," "I guess," "sometimes I wonder"
- Sharing personal stories or vulnerable details
- Making connections between behavior and consequences
- Humor (genuine, not sarcastic)

### How to Respond to Shifts

**When defensiveness rises:** Pull back immediately. Reflect what they're feeling. Emphasize autonomy. Drop the topic if needed. Trust that the seed has been planted.

**When defensiveness lowers:** Move gently. Don't rush to capitalize on the opening. Let them lead. Explore with gentle curiosity. Affirm their openness without making a big deal of it.

---

## Defensiveness Response Decision Tree

1. **Is the client hostile or shut down?** Use amplified reflection, shift focus, or come alongside. Do NOT push.

2. **Is the client minimizing or rationalizing?** Use double-sided reflections or the Columbo approach. Explore gently.

3. **Is the client open and reflective?** Strengthen change talk, evoke deeper motivation, affirm strengths.

4. **Has defensiveness just increased?** Stop what you're doing. Reflect the emotion. Emphasize autonomy. Let them breathe.

5. **Has defensiveness just decreased?** Follow their lead. Ask a gentle open question. Don't rush.

---

## Core Principle

Defensiveness is not the client's problem. It is information about the therapeutic relationship. When defensiveness is high, the therapist should look at what they are doing, not what the client is doing. The client is responding to something - perceived judgment, loss of autonomy, shame, fear. The therapist's job is to remove the source of threat, not to push through it.
