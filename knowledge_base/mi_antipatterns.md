# MI Antipatterns: Common Mistakes to Avoid

Motivational Interviewing is as much about what you do NOT do as what you do. These antipatterns are common traps that undermine the therapeutic relationship, increase defensiveness, and move the client away from change. Recognizing and avoiding them is essential.

---

## 1. The Righting Reflex

### What It Looks Like

The therapist feels a strong urge to fix the client's problem, correct their thinking, or steer them toward the "right" answer. The therapist provides solutions, argues for change, or explains why the client should behave differently.

- "You really should think about cutting back."
- "Have you considered that drinking is making everything worse?"
- "The solution is obvious - you need to stop drinking on weeknights."
- Jumping to problem-solving before the client has fully explored their own ambivalence.

### Why It Fails

When a therapist argues for change, the client argues against it. This is a basic principle of human psychology - when someone pushes, we push back. The righting reflex puts the therapist in the role of advocate for change, which paradoxically pushes the client into defending the status quo. It also communicates that the therapist knows better, which undermines the client's autonomy and the collaborative spirit of MI.

### What to Do Instead

Let the client make the argument for change. Your job is to evoke their reasons, not supply your own. Ask questions that help them explore their ambivalence. Reflect their change talk back to them so they hear it more clearly.

### Example: Bad vs. Good Response

Client: "I know I drink too much, but it helps me deal with stress."

**Bad (Righting Reflex):** "But there are much healthier ways to deal with stress. Exercise, meditation, talking to someone - all of those are better options. Drinking is actually making your stress worse in the long run."

**Good (MI-Consistent):** "Drinking serves an important purpose for you right now - it's how you manage the stress. And at the same time, you said you know it's too much. What does 'too much' look like for you?"

## 2. Premature Advice Giving

### What It Looks Like

The therapist offers solutions, suggestions, or recommendations before the client is ready to hear them, before they've asked for them, or before the problem has been fully explored.

- "You should try going to AA meetings."
- "What you need to do is find a hobby to replace the drinking."
- "Here's what I'd recommend..."
- Launching into a plan when the client is still in pre-contemplation or early contemplation.

### Why It Fails

Unsolicited advice triggers reactance - the client feels their autonomy is being threatened and resists. Even good advice fails when it arrives at the wrong time. It also signals that the therapist has stopped listening and started prescribing. The client may agree superficially ("yeah, maybe") while internally rejecting the suggestion and becoming less likely to bring up concerns in the future.

### What to Do Instead

Ask permission before sharing information: "Would it be okay if I shared something?" Wait until the client expresses readiness or asks directly. When you do share, use the Elicit-Provide-Elicit framework: ask what they already know, provide information with permission, then ask what they make of it.

### Example: Bad vs. Good Response

Client: "I've been drinking every night for the past month."

**Bad (Premature Advice):** "You should try to go three nights a week without drinking. Start a journal to track your triggers. And look into a support group in your area."

**Good (MI-Consistent):** "Every night for a month. What's that been like for you?"

## 3. Confrontation and Labeling

### What It Looks Like

The therapist directly challenges the client's behavior, uses diagnostic labels, or tries to force the client to accept a particular identity or reality.

- "You're an alcoholic and you need to accept that."
- "You're in denial about how bad this has gotten."
- "Let's be honest - this IS a problem."
- "The fact that you can't stop proves you're dependent."
- Using clinical language the client hasn't used themselves.

### Why It Fails

Labels trigger shame and defensiveness. When someone is told they are an "alcoholic" or "in denial," their natural response is to reject the label and the person applying it. Confrontation creates an adversarial dynamic that destroys the collaborative relationship MI depends on. Even if the label is clinically accurate, forcing it shuts down exploration and pushes the client away.

### What to Do Instead

Follow the client's language. If they say "I drink too much," stay with "drinking too much." Avoid all diagnostic labels unless the client introduces them. Focus on behavior and consequences, not identity. Replace confrontation with curiosity.

### Example: Bad vs. Good Response

Client: "I sometimes drink more than I mean to."

**Bad (Confrontation/Labeling):** "That's called loss of control, which is a classic sign of alcohol dependency. I think we need to be honest about what's going on here."

**Good (MI-Consistent):** "You set out to have a certain amount and then find yourself going past that. What happens in those moments?"

## 4. The Expert Trap

### What It Looks Like

The therapist positions themselves as the authority who has the answers, rather than as a collaborative partner. They lecture, educate excessively, or communicate (verbally or nonverbally) that they know what's best for the client.

- "Research shows that people who drink as much as you have a higher risk of..."
- "In my experience, what works best is..."
- Talking more than the client
- Providing long explanations without checking in
- Assuming you understand the client's experience better than they do

### Why It Fails

MI is grounded in the principle that the client is the expert on their own life. When the therapist takes the expert role, the client becomes passive - either complying superficially or withdrawing entirely. The power imbalance undermines the partnership that drives change. Clients stop sharing openly because they feel they're being assessed rather than understood.

### What to Do Instead

Be genuinely curious. Ask more than you tell. When you do share information, frame it as one perspective, not the truth. Use phrases like "I wonder..." and "What do you think about...?" Let the client lead the direction of the conversation. Your role is to walk alongside them, not in front of them.

### Example: Bad vs. Good Response

Client: "I don't really know much about how alcohol affects the body."

**Bad (Expert Trap):** "Well, let me explain. Alcohol is processed by the liver, and when you drink heavily over time, it causes fatty liver disease, which can progress to cirrhosis. Your BAC rises about 0.02 per drink, and anything over 0.08 is legally impaired. The recommended limits are..." [continues for three paragraphs]

**Good (MI-Consistent):** "What have you noticed in your own body - any changes you've wondered about? ... [after listening] Would it be okay if I shared a couple of things that might be relevant to what you're describing?"

## 5. The Yes-But Pattern

### What It Looks Like

The therapist acknowledges the client's statement but immediately follows with a counterargument. The word "but" functions as an eraser - everything before it is negated.

- "I understand you enjoy drinking, BUT it's causing real problems."
- "That's a valid point, BUT have you considered..."
- "I hear what you're saying, BUT the reality is..."
- "You're right that your friends drink too, BUT that doesn't mean it's okay for you."

### Why It Fails

The client hears the "but" and knows that what follows is what the therapist really thinks. Everything before "but" was performative acknowledgment. This creates a pattern where the client feels heard for one second and then overridden. Over time, the client stops sharing honestly because they know their perspective will be "but-ted" away. It's a subtle form of the righting reflex.

### What to Do Instead

Replace "but" with "and." This small linguistic shift keeps both realities alive without one negating the other. Alternatively, use two separate reflections. Or simply let the client's statement stand without immediately challenging it.

### Example: Bad vs. Good Response

Client: "Drinking helps me be more social. I'm really shy without it."

**Bad (Yes-But):** "I understand it helps your social confidence, but relying on alcohol for that is really just masking the anxiety and making it worse over time."

**Good (MI-Consistent):** "Drinking does something important for you socially - it lowers that barrier that shyness puts up. And you've noticed that connection matters enough to you that you've found a way to make it happen. What would it be like if you could feel that way without the drinks?"

## Quick Reference: Antipattern Detection

| If you notice yourself... | You may be falling into... | Try instead... |
|---|---|---|
| Arguing for change | Righting Reflex | Ask an open question |
| Offering solutions unprompted | Premature Advice | Explore their perspective first |
| Using clinical labels | Confrontation | Use the client's own language |
| Talking more than the client | Expert Trap | Reflect and ask |
| Saying "I hear you, but..." | Yes-But Pattern | Replace "but" with "and" |

---

## The Underlying Principle

All five antipatterns share a common root: the therapist is working harder than the client. In MI, if you find yourself pushing, persuading, advising, or correcting, something has gone wrong. The client should be the one voicing arguments for change. Your job is to create the conditions where that becomes possible.
