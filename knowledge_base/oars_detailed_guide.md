# OARS Detailed Technique Guide

OARS stands for Open Questions, Affirmations, Reflections, and Summaries. These are the core micro-skills of Motivational Interviewing. This guide provides detailed examples organized by situation and type.

---

## Open Questions

Open questions invite exploration, elaboration, and self-reflection. They cannot be answered with a simple yes or no. The therapist uses them strategically to evoke change talk, explore ambivalence, and deepen understanding.

---

### Exploring Ambivalence

Use these when the client is weighing both sides of change.

1. "What are some of the good things about drinking, and what are some of the not-so-good things?"
2. "How do you see your drinking fitting into the life you want?"
3. "What would you miss most if you made a change, and what would you gain?"
4. "On one hand you enjoy drinking, and on the other it's causing some problems. Where does that leave you?"
5. "If you could have it both ways - keep the good parts and lose the bad - what would that look like?"

---

### Evoking Change Talk

Use these to draw out the client's own reasons and motivation for change.

1. "What concerns you most about your drinking right now?"
2. "If you decided to make a change, what would your reasons be?"
3. "What would be different in your life a year from now if you cut back on drinking?"
4. "On a scale from 0 to 10, how important is it for you to change your drinking? What makes it a [number] and not a zero?"
5. "What would it take for you to feel ready to do something different?"

---

### Exploring Values

Use these to connect behavior change to the client's deeper values and identity.

1. "What matters most to you in life right now?"
2. "How does your drinking line up with the kind of parent you want to be?"
3. "When you think about the person you want to be, what comes to mind?"
4. "What would your best self say about how things are going?"
5. "If your [child/partner/friend] could see everything, what would you want them to see?"

---

## Affirmations

Affirmations recognize the client's strengths, efforts, and values. They build confidence and reinforce the therapeutic relationship. Effective affirmations are specific, genuine, and focused on the person rather than outcomes.

---

### Strength-Based Affirmations

Recognizing inherent qualities and capabilities.

1. "You're someone who doesn't give up easily. That says a lot about your character."
2. "The way you think about this shows real self-awareness."
3. "You've survived some incredibly difficult things. There's a resilience in you that's hard to ignore."
4. "You have a clear sense of what matters to you. That's a real strength."
5. "Even when things are hard, you keep showing up. That takes something."

---

### Effort-Based Affirmations

Recognizing actions, attempts, and commitment regardless of outcome.

1. "You came here today even though it wasn't easy. That took courage."
2. "The fact that you're even thinking about this tells me something important about you."
3. "You tried cutting back last week, and even though it didn't go the way you wanted, you tried. That matters."
4. "You've been paying attention to your patterns. Not everyone does that."
5. "You reached out when you were struggling. That's not a small thing."

---

### Value-Based Affirmations

Recognizing the client's core values and how they show up.

1. "It's clear how much you care about your kids. Everything you're saying comes back to them."
2. "Your honesty today - that's something I really respect. Not everyone can be this real about what's going on."
3. "You take your responsibilities seriously. That matters, even when it feels like you're falling short."
4. "The fact that this bothers you tells me your values are still very much intact."
5. "You're someone who wants to do right by the people in your life. That hasn't changed."

---

## Reflections

Reflections demonstrate that the therapist has heard and understood the client. They come in two forms: simple and complex. Reflections are the most frequently used MI skill and should outnumber questions roughly 2:1.

---

### Simple Reflections

Repeat, rephrase, or slightly reword what the client said. Useful for building rapport and ensuring the client feels heard.

1. Client: "I drink most nights after work."
   Therapist: "Drinking has become part of your evening routine."

2. Client: "My wife keeps telling me I drink too much."
   Therapist: "Your wife has been expressing concern about your drinking."

3. Client: "I don't think it's that big of a deal."
   Therapist: "You don't see it as a problem right now."

4. Client: "I only drink beer, not hard liquor."
   Therapist: "You feel like sticking to beer keeps things in control."

5. Client: "I've tried cutting back before but it never sticks."
   Therapist: "You've made efforts to change, and it hasn't lasted the way you wanted."

6. Client: "Everyone at my job drinks. It's just what we do."
   Therapist: "Drinking is a big part of the culture at work."

7. Client: "I don't want to be here."
   Therapist: "This isn't where you'd choose to be."

8. Client: "Some mornings I feel terrible."
   Therapist: "The mornings have been rough."

9. Client: "I guess I'm a little worried about my health."
   Therapist: "There's some concern about how this might be affecting your body."

10. Client: "My kids have started noticing."
    Therapist: "Your children are picking up on what's going on."

---

### Complex Reflections

Go beyond what was said to add meaning, feeling, or connection. They reflect the emotion beneath the words, make an educated guess about what the client means, or highlight ambivalence.

1. Client: "I don't have a problem. I just drink to relax."
   Therapist: "Drinking serves an important purpose for you - it's how you decompress. And the idea that it could be a problem feels like it doesn't fit with how you experience it."

2. Client: "My mom was an alcoholic. I'm nothing like her."
   Therapist: "There's something frightening about even the possibility of a comparison. You've worked hard to be different."

3. Client: "I missed my kid's game because I was hungover. I felt like garbage."
   Therapist: "That moment hit you hard - not just the hangover, but what it cost you with your child. Being there for them matters deeply to you."

4. Client: "I keep saying I'll cut back and then I don't."
   Therapist: "There's a gap between what you want to do and what ends up happening, and that gap is frustrating. Part of you is ready for something different."

5. Client: "Everyone's making this into a bigger deal than it is."
   Therapist: "All this attention and concern feels out of proportion to what you're experiencing. It might even feel unfair."

6. Client: "I don't know why I keep doing this to myself."
   Therapist: "You're asking yourself a hard question. Something about the pattern doesn't match who you want to be."

7. Client: "Maybe I should just quit entirely. Cold turkey."
   Therapist: "You're at a point where half-measures don't feel like enough. You want something decisive."

8. Client: "My doctor said my liver numbers are high. Whatever."
   Therapist: "You heard the news and brushed it off on the outside. I'm wondering if there's a part of you that didn't brush it off quite so easily."

9. Client: "I was sober for three months and I felt amazing. Then I blew it."
   Therapist: "You got a taste of what life could feel like without alcohol, and you liked it. And now there's grief about losing that."

10. Client: "What's the point? I've tried everything."
    Therapist: "You're exhausted from trying. And the idea of putting in effort again, only to end up back here, feels unbearable."

---

### Double-Sided Reflections

Capture both sides of the client's ambivalence using an "and" or "on one hand... on the other" structure. Especially powerful for helping clients hear their own conflicting motivations.

1. Client: "I love going out with my friends, but I always overdo it."
   Therapist: "The social connection means a lot to you, and at the same time, you've noticed that the drinking part goes further than you want it to."

2. Client: "I know I should stop, but it helps me sleep."
   Therapist: "On one hand, you can see the reasons to stop. On the other, alcohol is solving a real problem for you right now - getting rest."

3. Client: "My wife is threatening to leave, but I'm not an alcoholic."
   Therapist: "Your marriage is at stake, and that matters to you. And you also feel like the label doesn't fit who you are."

4. Client: "I want to be healthy, but I don't want to give up my social life."
   Therapist: "Health is important to you, and so are your friendships and your social world. You're not sure you can have both."

5. Client: "Part of me wants to change and part of me just doesn't care."
   Therapist: "There's a part that sees the value in doing something different, and a part that's tired of caring about it. Both of those are real."

---

## Summaries

Summaries collect what the client has said and present it back in an organized way. They demonstrate deep listening, highlight change talk, and help the client see the bigger picture.

---

### Collecting Summaries

Gather what's been said so far in the conversation. Used during or at the end of a discussion.

1. "Let me pull together what I've heard so far. You came in today because your doctor mentioned your liver numbers, and that got your attention even though you're not sure it's a big deal. You've noticed that you're drinking most nights, and the mornings have been harder. Your wife has said something about it too. And underneath all of that, there's a part of you that wonders if things have gotten a little out of hand."

2. "So here's what I'm hearing. You started drinking more during the pandemic, and it just kind of stuck. You've noticed it's affecting your sleep, your energy, and your mood. At the same time, it's still the thing that helps you unwind at the end of the day. You're not sure what you'd replace it with."

3. "Let me see if I've got this right. You're a dad who wants to be present for his kids. You've been drinking more than you'd like. You had a scare last month when you blacked out, and your daughter saw something that still bothers you. And you're sitting here wondering what to do about it."

---

### Linking Summaries

Connect what's being said now to something the client mentioned earlier, showing patterns and themes.

1. "You mentioned earlier that your dad drank a lot when you were growing up, and just now you said you're worried about what your own kids see. It sounds like there's a pattern you're determined not to repeat."

2. "Last time we talked, you said drinking helps you deal with the stress from work. Today you mentioned that the stress has actually gotten worse, and so has the drinking. Those two things seem connected."

3. "A few minutes ago you talked about how proud you felt during those sober weeks. And now you're telling me about the shame after last weekend. Both of those feelings seem to point to the same thing - this matters to you more than you sometimes let on."

4. "Earlier you said you drink because everyone around you does. But just now you said your closest friend actually stopped last year and seems happier. I'm curious what you make of that."

5. "You started today saying you don't have a problem. But as we've been talking, you've mentioned your health, your wife's concerns, the missed game, and the mornings that feel rough. There's quite a bit on your mind."

---

### Transitional Summaries

Used to shift the conversation from one phase to another - often from exploring the problem to exploring solutions. They wrap up one topic and open the door to the next.

1. "So we've spent some time talking about how drinking fits into your life - the stress relief, the social side, the unwinding after work. And we've also talked about the costs - the mornings, your wife's worry, the health numbers. Given all of that, where would you like to go from here?"

2. "You've painted a pretty clear picture today. The drinking started as something manageable and has become something that's taking more than it gives. You've got some real reasons to think about change. What would you want to explore next?"

3. "Let me gather up what we've covered. You're at a point where the drinking is bumping up against things you care about - your kids, your health, your work. You've tried making changes before, and you know it's not easy. You're not sure you're ready for a big change, but you're also not sure you want things to stay the same. If it's okay with you, I'd like to talk about what a small step might look like."

4. "So here's where we are. You've been honest about what's going on, and that's not nothing. You can see both sides - the reasons to keep things as they are and the reasons to try something different. Before we wrap up today, I'm wondering - is there one thing, however small, that you'd be willing to try this week?"

5. "We've covered a lot of ground today. You walked in not sure this was the right place for you, and you've ended up sharing some things that clearly matter. I appreciate your openness. As we move toward wrapping up, what's standing out to you from our conversation?"
