# Motivational Interviewing (MI) Core Principles

## The Spirit of MI

Motivational Interviewing is a collaborative, goal-oriented style of communication with particular attention to the language of change. It is designed to strengthen personal motivation for and commitment to a specific goal by eliciting and exploring the person's own reasons for change within an atmosphere of acceptance and compassion.

### Four Key Elements (PACE):

1. **Partnership**: MI is done "with" and "for" someone, not "to" or "on" them. The counselor is a partner, not an expert telling them what to do.

2. **Acceptance**: Absolute worth - every person has inherent value. Autonomy support - people have the right to make their own choices. Accurate empathy - understanding their perspective. Affirmation - acknowledging strengths and efforts.

3. **Compassion**: Actively promoting the other person's welfare and prioritizing their needs.

4. **Evocation**: Drawing out the person's own motivations and resources, rather than imposing external ones.

## The Four Processes of MI

### 1. Engaging

Building a working relationship based on trust and mutual respect. Without engagement, the other processes cannot happen effectively.

- Use OARS skills (Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections, Summaries)
- Show genuine interest
- Avoid the "expert trap"
- Be curious about their perspective

### 2. Focusing

Finding a clear direction for the conversation about change.

- Identify what matters to them
- Clarify the target behavior
- Maintain focus without being rigid

### 3. Evoking

Eliciting the person's own motivations for change (change talk).

- Ask evocative questions
- Explore importance and confidence
- Look forward and look back
- Query extremes
- Use the Readiness Ruler

### 4. Planning

Developing commitment to change and a concrete plan (when the person is ready).

- Only when there's sufficient motivation
- Collaborative, not prescriptive
- Practical and realistic
- Include strategies for challenges

## OARS: Core Skills

### Open Questions

Questions that cannot be answered with yes/no. They invite elaboration and exploration.

Examples:

- "What brings you here today?"
- "How do you feel about your drinking?"
- "What would be different if you made a change?"

### Affirmations

Statements that recognize client strengths, efforts, and values.

Examples:

- "You've shown real courage in talking about this."
- "It's clear how much you care about your family."
- "Coming here today took a lot of strength."

### Reflections

Statements that demonstrate you've heard and understood. They can be simple (repeating) or complex (adding meaning or feeling).

Simple reflection: "You feel frustrated."
Complex reflection: "It sounds like you're torn between wanting to keep drinking and wanting to be healthier."

### Summaries

Collecting what's been said and presenting it back. Shows you've been listening and can highlight change talk.

Example: "Let me see if I understand. You've been drinking more lately, and you're worried about how it's affecting your work and your relationship with your kids. At the same time, you feel like drinking helps you relax after stressful days..."
