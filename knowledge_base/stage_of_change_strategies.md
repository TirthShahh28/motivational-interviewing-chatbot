# Stages of Change: MI Strategies for Each Stage

Prochaska and DiClemente's Transtheoretical Model describes five stages people move through when changing behavior. Effective MI requires matching your approach to the client's current stage. Pushing someone toward action when they're in pre-contemplation creates resistance. Meeting them where they are builds trust and momentum.

---

## Stage 1: Pre-Contemplation

### Description

The client does not see their drinking as a problem or is not considering change. They may be unaware of consequences, in denial, or feel that others are overreacting. They often arrive in therapy due to external pressure (partner, employer, court, doctor).

### Therapist Goals

- Build rapport and trust without pushing an agenda
- Plant seeds of doubt gently through exploration
- Avoid arguing or trying to convince
- Raise awareness by exploring the client's own experiences
- Respect autonomy absolutely

### Strategies

- Focus on engagement, not change
- Use open questions to explore their perspective, not to lead them somewhere
- Provide information only when permission is given ("Would it be okay if I shared something?")
- Express empathy for their situation, including the pressure they feel from others
- Avoid labeling (alcoholic, problem drinker, addict)
- Highlight discrepancies gently if the client introduces them

### Key Phrases

- "You're not sure drinking is really a problem."
- "It sounds like other people are more concerned than you are."
- "What do you enjoy about drinking?"
- "What would have to happen for you to become concerned?"
- "You're the best judge of what's going on in your life."

### Example Exchanges

**Exchange 1:**
Client: "I'm only here because my wife made me come. I don't have a drinking problem."
Therapist: "This wasn't your idea. You're here because your wife is worried, and you're not sure there's anything to worry about. Help me understand - what does she see that concerns her?"

**Exchange 2:**
Client: "Everyone drinks. I don't drink more than my friends."
Therapist: "Among your friend group, the drinking feels pretty normal. I'm curious - setting aside what everyone else does, have you ever noticed anything about your own drinking that made you pause, even for a second?"

**Exchange 3:**
Client: "My doctor told me to cut back, but doctors say that to everyone."
Therapist: "You feel like it's standard advice, not necessarily aimed at you specifically. What did the doctor mention that prompted the recommendation?"

## Stage 2: Contemplation

### Description

The client is aware that drinking may be causing problems and is weighing the pros and cons of change. They feel ambivalent - they can see reasons to change and reasons to stay the same. They are not yet committed to action but are thinking about it.

### Therapist Goals

- Explore and deepen ambivalence without resolving it for them
- Tip the decisional balance by eliciting change talk
- Help the client hear their own arguments for change
- Build confidence that change is possible
- Avoid pushing toward action prematurely

### Strategies

- Use double-sided reflections to capture both sides
- Explore the pros and cons of drinking and of changing
- Ask evocative questions about what change could look like
- Use the importance and confidence rulers
- Summarize change talk and reflect it back
- Look forward: "Where do you see yourself in five years if nothing changes?"
- Look back: "What was life like before drinking became a concern?"

### Key Phrases

- "You're seeing both sides of this."
- "Part of you wants things to be different."
- "What's the most important reason you can see for making a change?"
- "What gives you some confidence that you could change if you wanted to?"
- "It sounds like you're weighing some important things right now."

### Example Exchanges

**Exchange 1:**
Client: "I know I should probably cut back. But honestly, after the week I have, I feel like I've earned it."
Therapist: "So there are two things that are both true - you can see reasons to drink less, and at the same time, drinking fills a real need in your life right now. It's how you reward yourself after pushing through hard weeks. What would cutting back cost you?"

**Exchange 2:**
Client: "My health numbers aren't great. But my dad drank his whole life and he's fine."
Therapist: "You're looking at the evidence and finding arguments on both sides. Your doctor is concerned, and your dad's experience tells a different story. I'm curious - when you picture yourself at your dad's age, what do you want your health to look like?"

**Exchange 3:**
Client: "Some days I think I should quit. Other days I think everyone's overreacting."
Therapist: "It shifts depending on the day. On the days when quitting feels right, what's happening that makes you think that way?"

## Stage 3: Preparation

### Description

The client has decided to make a change and is getting ready to take action. They may be researching options, setting a date, telling others about their intentions, or making small preliminary changes. Motivation is building but has not yet been tested.

### Therapist Goals

- Support and strengthen their commitment without over-planning
- Help them develop a realistic, concrete plan
- Anticipate challenges and discuss strategies
- Build self-efficacy by highlighting past successes
- Ensure the plan is theirs, not yours

### Strategies

- Affirm their decision and the courage it took
- Help identify specific, achievable first steps
- Explore potential barriers and triggers
- Develop coping strategies for high-risk situations
- Identify support systems (people, groups, resources)
- Keep the plan flexible - rigid plans often fail
- Discuss what they'll do if they slip

### Key Phrases

- "You've made a decision, and that's a big step."
- "What feels like the right first move for you?"
- "What situations might make it harder to stick with your plan?"
- "Who in your life would support you in this?"
- "What's worked for you before when you've made a difficult change?"

### Example Exchanges

**Exchange 1:**
Client: "I've decided I'm not going to drink during the week anymore. Starting Monday."
Therapist: "You've drawn a clear line, and you're ready. Let's think this through together. Monday evening comes, it's been a long day - what's your plan for that moment when you'd normally pour a drink?"

**Exchange 2:**
Client: "I told my wife I'm going to try cutting back. She was relieved. But I'm nervous."
Therapist: "You took a step that mattered - you told someone important to you. And the nervousness makes sense. You're about to change something that's been a big part of your life. What part of it makes you most nervous?"

**Exchange 3:**
Client: "I cleared out the liquor from the house. I'm keeping a couple of beers for the weekend only."
Therapist: "You're already taking action - that tells me you're serious about this. You've thought about what structure works for you. What will you do if the weekend beers start creeping into the week?"

## Stage 4: Action

### Description

The client is actively making changes to their drinking behavior. This is the most visible stage but also the most demanding. They are modifying habits, coping with urges, navigating social situations differently, and discovering what life looks like without relying on alcohol.

### Therapist Goals

- Provide consistent support and encouragement
- Help process the challenges that come with change
- Reinforce their motivation by connecting daily effort to values
- Normalize difficulty without minimizing it
- Help identify and manage triggers in real time
- Celebrate progress without creating pressure to perform

### Strategies

- Affirm effort over outcome - the work matters regardless of perfection
- Process each challenge as a learning opportunity
- Explore what's working and what's not without judgment
- Revisit and adjust the plan as needed
- Help them find alternative coping strategies and rewards
- Monitor for signs of burnout, isolation, or white-knuckling
- Gently explore whether additional support might be helpful

### Key Phrases

- "You're doing this. Day by day."
- "How has this week felt compared to what you expected?"
- "What's been the hardest part so far?"
- "What helped you get through that tough moment?"
- "You chose differently this time. What made that possible?"

### Example Exchanges

**Exchange 1:**
Client: "It's been two weeks. Weeknights have been fine, but the weekends are brutal. Everyone's out drinking and I'm just... sitting there."
Therapist: "Two weeks is real. And the weekends are showing you something important - how much your social life was built around drinking. That loneliness and awkwardness you're feeling makes complete sense. How are you handling those moments?"

**Exchange 2:**
Client: "I almost drank last night. I was standing in front of the fridge for like five minutes. But I didn't."
Therapist: "Five minutes is a long time to stand in front of that decision. And you made a choice that was hard. What was going on inside you during those five minutes?"

**Exchange 3:**
Client: "I've been sleeping better. And my mornings are actually... good? Like I forgot what it feels like to not be foggy."
Therapist: "You're experiencing some of the benefits firsthand now. Clear mornings, better sleep. Those aren't small things. What else have you noticed?"

## Stage 5: Maintenance and Relapse

### Description

The client has sustained changes over time and is working to maintain them. The risk of relapse is always present, especially during high-stress periods, life transitions, or when the initial motivation fades. If relapse occurs, the client may cycle back to contemplation, preparation, or action.

### Therapist Goals

- Normalize the ongoing nature of change - maintenance is not passive
- Help identify early warning signs of relapse
- Build long-term coping strategies and lifestyle changes
- Process relapse without shame if it occurs
- Reinforce identity as someone who has changed, not someone in constant recovery
- Support gradual transition to independence from therapy

### Strategies

- Review what's working and reinforce sustainable habits
- Discuss high-risk situations that may emerge (holidays, stress, life events)
- Help build a relapse prevention plan: triggers, warning signs, action steps
- If relapse occurs: validate, explore what happened, reconnect to motivation
- Avoid treating relapse as failure - frame it as information
- Celebrate milestones without creating a pass/fail mentality
- Explore what life after therapy looks like

### Key Phrases

- "Change isn't a destination - it's something you keep choosing."
- "What helps you stay on track during the harder weeks?"
- "If you had a tough night, what would you want to do the next morning?"
- "You've built something real. Let's talk about how to protect it."
- "A setback doesn't erase everything you've done."

### Example Exchanges

**Exchange 1:**
Client: "I've been sober for six months. But my college roommate's wedding is next month and I'm already stressing about the open bar."
Therapist: "Six months is significant. And you're doing something smart - you're thinking about a high-risk situation before you're in it. Let's map it out. What are the trickiest moments you can anticipate at the wedding?"

**Exchange 2:**
Client: "I had a beer at the barbecue. Just one. But I feel terrible about it."
Therapist: "One beer after six months of sobriety, and you stopped at one. The guilt tells me how much this matters to you. But I'm curious - what do you think that one beer tells you? Is it a slip, or is it a sign of something you need to address?"

**Exchange 3:**
Client: "I'm starting to wonder if I even need to keep coming here. Things are good."
Therapist: "Things are good, and that's a testament to the work you've done. The fact that you're wondering about stepping back from therapy - that actually makes sense at this stage. What would feel right to you? And what would you want to have in place so you feel supported even if we're not meeting regularly?"
